Not every app wants your words the same way. In a terminal you want exactly what you said, and Return shouldn't run a command before you've read it. In a chat, Return should send the message. At work, post-processing may have to go through your company's provider; at home, through your own. A profile holds such differences for one kind of work, and DanyWhere can choose it by the app you dictate into. This chapter shows how profiles and the Defaults work together, so you know where a change applies before you make it.

## The Defaults hold everything, a profile only what differs

Under **Defaults** in the sidebar you find every setting that shapes a dictation, on the pages **General**, **Models**, **Post Process** and **Advanced**. A new installation dictates with the Defaults alone; you need a profile only once something should be different.

A profile lies on top of the Defaults. Its pages look exactly like theirs, but it holds only its own values, and everywhere else the default shows through. Change a default, and every profile without its own value there follows at once. Change a value inside a profile, and only that profile changes.

## A profile's own values

Open a profile under **Profile Settings** in the sidebar, for example **Profile Settings › Terminal › Advanced**. Under every setting, a line says where its value comes from:

- A whole chain and a grey **Default**: the setting follows the Defaults.
- A broken chain and **Own value** in petrol: the profile keeps this value, whatever happens to the default. **Default: …** names the value of the Defaults, or **same as the default** says that the two happen to agree. **Reset** lets the setting follow the Defaults again.

Post Processing, switched off, shows a broken chain, Own value in petrol, Default: on, and a Reset button."
  caption="Inside a profile, every setting says whether it follows the Defaults."

Change a setting that follows the default, and it becomes the profile's own value. A bar at the bottom says so, for example **“Post Processing” now applies only to “Terminal”.** If you meant every profile, click **Change the Default Instead**: the Defaults take the new value, and the profile follows them again. **Undo** puts the default back.

**Page to Defaults** at the top of a page resets all its own values. The profile's first page, **Profile**, lists everything it holds of its own under **Own Values**: a click on a row opens that setting, the round arrow resets just that one, and **Whole Profile to Defaults** resets them all, keeping the profile's name and apps. Before resetting more than one value, DanyWhere asks and names them. In the sidebar, a number next to a profile and its pages counts their own values.

:::tip[Keep a value that happens to be the same]
To keep a value that the Defaults have too, such as post-processing switched off for your terminal, click the whole chain. The value becomes the profile's own without changing, and it stays when you change the default later. A click on the broken chain lets it follow again.
:::

## Seen from the Defaults

You can also work the other way round, one setting at a time. On the pages under **Defaults**, a setting that a profile does differently lists that profile underneath, with its own control and a round arrow that resets it. For larger settings, such as the prompts, the word lists or the end shortcuts, a summary stands in for the control, and **Open in Terminal** takes you to the setting inside the profile. A grey line names the profiles that follow, or says **All profiles follow**, and **+ Profile** gives one more profile its own value, starting from the default.

The pages under **Defaults** always show and change the Defaults, whichever profile is in use.

## Lists, entry by entry

Some settings are lists: the **End Shortcuts** and the other shortcuts, the **Additional Lists** of words, and the post-processing providers with their models. A profile differs from such a list entry by entry: it changes an entry, adds one, or switches off one of the Defaults' entries. The state line marks each of them **own** or **off**, with a reset of its own. All other entries keep following the Defaults, including entries you add there later. On a Mac, a profile for your chats could send with <kbd>↩</kbd> this way: it changes only **Direct · Paragraph**, so that it presses Enter instead of starting a new paragraph.

Words and prompts work a little differently. The **Word List** of the Defaults applies in every profile; a profile can replace it as a whole, but usually it adds **Additional Words** in front of it, which only it uses. The prompts belong to all profiles, and which of them are **Active**, in what order, belongs to the profile.

## Which profile a dictation uses

What dictations use is chosen in the box **In Use** at the top of the sidebar, or under **Profile** in the tray menu: the **Defaults** or one of your profiles. This decides only what your dictations use, not which settings you edit.

Profiles do the most when DanyWhere chooses them for you. Give a profile the apps it's meant for, and leave **Choose Profile by Application** on, as it is out of the box. A dictation into one of those apps then uses that profile; everywhere else, what **In Use** shows applies.

/>

The app in front **when the recording ends** decides, since that's where the text goes. You can start a dictation anywhere and switch to your chat before you end it. If you later insert the text into another app with **Paste Last Transcript**, DanyWhere redoes what that app's profile does differently: it transcribes again with another speech model, post-processes again, or only appends something else. **Copy Last Transcript** keeps the dictation's profile, and a dictation you've edited in the **History** keeps your text.

While a profile applies, the overlay names it during the dictation; with the Defaults, it shows no name.

To switch choosing by application off for a while, without taking any app away from its profile, use the switch on **Profile Settings**, **By application** under **In Use**, the tray menu, or the shortcut **Choose Profile by Application**: <kbd>⌃⇧A</kbd> on a Mac, <kbd>Alt+Shift+A</kbd> on Windows. The overlay then briefly shows whether choosing is on or off, and which profile a dictation here would get. While it's off, the profile under **In Use** applies everywhere, and the tray icon shows a padlock.

A change from the tray or a shortcut, such as switching to the next word list, goes where the value comes from: into the profile in use if it has its own value there, otherwise into the Defaults.

### Give a profile its apps

<Tabs syncKey="os">
<TabItem label="macOS">

In the profile's row under **Profile Settings**, or on its page **Profile**, click **Add application…** and choose the app: running apps first, then the installed ones. An app belongs to one profile at most; one that's already in another profile says so, and moves.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Windows">

Type the program's file name, such as `WindowsTerminal.exe`, and click **Add**. Write it exactly as the Task Manager shows it under **Details**, capitals included. A program belongs to one profile at most.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Linux">

On Linux, DanyWhere can't tell yet which app is in front, so apps you give a profile don't choose it. Choose the profile under **In Use** or in the tray menu instead.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## What is the same for every profile

Some settings concern DanyWhere itself rather than a dictation. They're under **Application Settings** and stay the same whatever profile applies: starting with your computer, the tray icon, the language and look of the interface, updates, storage, and the page **Accessibility**. The **History** is shared too: it keeps the dictations of all profiles together.

## New, duplicate, delete, share

**Profile Settings** opens the overview: a row for the Defaults and one for each profile, with the apps each profile is used in, and **Choose Profile by Application** underneath.

<Screenshot
  name="profiles-overview"
  alt="The overview Profile Settings, group Profiles. A row for the Defaults, marked Active, then the profiles Terminal and Chat, each with its buttons. Under Terminal, Used automatically in: iTerm2 and Terminal; under Chat, Slack and Signal. Below the rows, the switch Choose Profile by Application, switched on, then the buttons New Profile and Import Profile."
  caption="The profile overview with two profiles and their apps."
/>

- **New Profile** makes a profile that follows the Defaults in everything.
- **Duplicate** copies a profile with its own values. A copy of the Defaults follows them in everything. The copy isn't put in use; choose it under **In Use** or with **Use**.
- **Delete** deletes a profile's settings and its own word lists; the prompts stay. You can delete every profile; the Defaults always stay.
- **Export** saves a profile to a file: every value it dictates with, its active prompts, its word lists and its apps. **Import Profile** turns such a file into a new profile, which follows your Defaults wherever its values equal them. An app that another profile already has stays there.

If the profile's post-processing provider has an API key, the export asks for a **Password** and encrypts the key with it. Importing the key needs the same password. Without it, **Import Without Key** brings in everything else, and the profile uses the key already entered on that computer, if there is one.

## Recipe: a profile for your terminal

<Steps>

1. Open **Profile Settings**, click **New Profile** and call it `Terminal`.

2. In the row of Terminal, click **Add application…** and choose Terminal or iTerm2. On Windows, type `WindowsTerminal.exe` and click **Add**. On Linux, choose **Terminal** under **In Use** while you work in the terminal, and skip the next step.

3. Check that **Choose Profile by Application** is on.

4. Open **Profile Settings › Terminal › Advanced** and find **Post Processing**. If it's on, switch it off. If it's already off, as it is out of the box, click the chain under it, so the terminal stays without post-processing even after you switch it on in the Defaults. The line under it now says **Own value**.

5. On a Mac, open **Profile Settings › Terminal › General**, scroll to **End Shortcuts** and empty the **Append** field of **Direct · Paragraph**. <kbd>↩</kbd> then ends a dictation in the terminal without a line break.

6. Try it: in the terminal, start a dictation, say a command and end it with <kbd>↩</kbd>. The overlay names **Terminal**, and the command appears as you said it, waiting for you to run it.

</Steps>
